# Monopoly lens
Score: 4/10 | Vote: PASS

## What is strong
- **Timing.** If the founder's figures hold, the window is open now. A large broker has agents trading for customers, its terms push the risk onto them, and supervisors and card networks are asking for this kind of layer.
- **A place in the execution path.** A proxy in front of a broker's existing MCP server, with no code change, is a credible way in. Whoever decides and records every agent action is hard to remove.
- **Switching costs from the record.** Once the log is the evidence a firm gives its supervisor, replacing Gate breaks its books and records. That is the plan's best claim to durability.
- **The seed of a network effect.** In front of many firms, Gate would see the same agent everywhere. An identity and track record that brokers accept could become a two-sided standard: developers certify because brokers accept it, and brokers accept it because agents carry it. Today this is a possibility, not a mechanism.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **Nothing proprietary in a crowded field.** Identity checks, a rules engine, pre-trade caps, a tamper-evident log and a kill switch are all well understood. None is ten times better than what a broker's own engineers could build. One person built the demo with an AI coding assistant, so a funded copycat can match it by month six, and open-sourcing the proxy gives away the on-ramp. The same buyer can reach Runlayer (MCP governance), Archestra (runtime guardrails) and AIUC (certification, overlapping Arena), and the platform vendors the plan wants as a channel could bundle it themselves. This points to a price fight.
2. **No small market to own.** The plan spans four products (Gate, the consumer app, Arena, AINRA) and four buyer segments, none narrow enough to dominate. How many firms will actually open accounts to agents in the next 18 months is unknown. Identity, Gate's first step, is where the card networks and better-funded identity companies are heading. A neutral AINRA cannot be Gate's moat.
3. **Distribution is unproven and expensive.** The buyer is a compliance or technology executive at a regulated firm, which means long cycles, security reviews and a SOC 2 report Gate does not have. There is no sales lead, no interviews and no design partner. Cost to acquire a customer and sales-cycle length are unknown, yet year one needs 13 paying firms, from zero conversations, on about $710,000.

## The questions I would ask the founder
- Name the first small market, count its firms, and say what share you will hold in 18 months.
- What do you know about agents trading real money that the funded neighbours do not?
- Against Runlayer, Archestra, the firm's own engineers and its platform vendor, why do you win, and why do you still win in month six?
- Which durable advantage are you building, how does it compound with each new firm, and what stays closed if the proxy is open source?
- If AINRA stays neutral and the card networks' standards win, what does Gate own?
- How do the first 100 customers arrive, what does each cost, and will any sign before SOC 2?
- Which of the four products do you stop?

## If FUND IF: the conditions
None: the vote is PASS. It would become FUND IF with:
1. One named, sized small market, with three design partners from it signed to letters of intent at a stated price.
2. A specific answer to the copycat question: the advantage that grows with each firm (most likely the cross-firm agent record) and how it stays proprietary.
3. The consumer app and Arena frozen as demos until Gate has a paying customer.
4. Someone on the team who has sold to regulated financial firms.
